Chapter I

Inheritance

She grew up around objects that had been appraised. Then she went and photographed people who refused to be.

Bettina Rheims was born in Neuilly-sur-Seine on 18 December 1952, into a household organised around the valuation of beautiful things. Her father, Maurice Rheims, was an auctioneer, an art historian and a member of the Académie française — a man whose profession was to look at an object, decide what it was worth, and say so out loud in a room full of people.

It is a useful thing to know about her, because the work that followed is almost entirely about the opposite transaction: a subject who sets the price, and a photographer who agrees to it.

§ 1 — 1978, and the rooms nobody photographed politely

She did not begin with fashion, and she did not begin with celebrities. She began in 1978 with strip-tease artists and acrobats — performers whose entire working life consisted of controlling exactly how much of themselves an audience got.

That is the whole method, arriving fully formed in the first series. The camera is not there to catch anyone out. It is there to record a negotiation that the subject has already won.

In 1982 came Animal, a series of taxidermied creatures: things posed to look alive by someone else's hand, staring back with glass. She has been circling the same question ever since — who arranged this, and how much of it is consent.

Her subjects are never caught. They are always agreeing.
The recurring finding of this monograph

§ 2 — Female Trouble, Modern Lovers

Female Trouble (1989) collected portraits of women. Modern Lovers (1989–90) went further and pulled at gender itself — androgynous young subjects photographed with a directness that read, at the time, as confrontational and now mostly reads as early.

The provocation was never the nudity. It was the eye contact.

Chapter II

Colour

A hotel room, a novelist, and a decision that black and white had become a way of flattering everybody.

Between 1990 and 1992 Rheims made Chambre Close with the novelist Serge Bramly, and it changed the register of everything after it. It was her first substantial body of work in colour, and the colour is not decorative — it is the point. Skin in a rented room under available light is specific in a way that monochrome quietly refuses to be.

Bramly did not simply write an introduction. The series arrived with a fiction attached: an invented photographer, a set of encounters, a frame around the images that made the viewer complicit in reading them as documents.

§ 1 — The collaborations

Bramly became a recurring co-author. In 1999 the two made I.N.R.I., a restaging of the life of Christ that was received exactly as you would expect a restaging of the life of Christ by this photographer to be received. In 2010 they made Rose, c'est Paris, a fictional narrative shot in and around the Bibliothèque nationale de France.

Between those: Shanghai (2002), made across two extended stays; Héroïnes (2005), where the designer Jean Colonna built garments that turned the sitters into sculpture; and Gender Studies (2012), portraits made with the composer Frédéric Sanchez in which sound was part of the record.

The through-line is that she almost never works alone, and the collaborator is almost never another photographer. It is a writer, a designer, a composer — someone whose job is to supply the frame she then photographs a person inside of.

The fiction is not a disguise. It is the permission slip.

Chapter III

State

The most-reproduced photograph she ever made is the one with the fewest choices left in it.

In 1995, after his election, the French Presidency commissioned Bettina Rheims to make the official portrait of Jacques Chirac. Set that sentence next to the previous chapter and it stops being a footnote: the Republic looked at a photographer known for Chambre Close and asked her to produce the image that would hang in every town hall in France.

The result is the most constrained commission imaginable — a format with centuries of rules, a subject who cannot be surprised, an audience of sixty million. And it is still recognisably hers, because the thing she does is the thing the genre needs: she lets the sitter decide what to hand over, and then she photographs precisely that.

§ 1 — What an official portrait is for

An official portrait is not a likeness. It is an instrument. It gets reproduced at every scale from a postage stamp to a hall banner, it has to survive being photocopied, and it has to say one thing without ambiguity for as long as the term lasts.

Which is why the commission is a genuine test rather than a curiosity. Everything Rheims normally uses — the room, the light she found there, the slow arrival at a pose — is unavailable. What is left is the negotiation itself.

§ 2 — The decorations, in order

The state kept asking. She was made an Officer of the Legion of Honour in 2006, promoted to Commander on 14 July 2013, and appointed Grand Officier de l'Ordre national du Mérite in 2018. Alongside that ran the commercial work — campaigns for Chanel and Lancôme, magazine portraiture — and the long list of sitters that gets quoted whenever her name appears: Kate Moss, Madonna, Monica Bellucci, Claudia Schiffer, Naomi Campbell.

France resolved the contradiction by simply declining to treat it as one.

Chapter IV

Mirror

Stockholm, 2016. A retrospective borrows its title from a song about being looked at.

In 2016 Fotografiska in Stockholm hung I'll Be Your Mirror — thirty-five years of the work in one building. The installation photographs on this page are all from that show, and they are the honest way to look at this body of work now: at scale, on a wall, with the room around them.

Seen that way the provocations flatten out and the consistency becomes obvious. It is the same photograph again and again across four decades — a person who has decided how they will be seen, looking directly back.

§ 1 — What the retrospective settles

A retrospective is a verdict on whether a career had a shape or merely a duration. Hung together, the answer here is not really in doubt — but it is not the answer the controversy suggested. The scandalous work and the state portrait turn out to be the same procedure applied under different constraints.

The title is borrowed from the Velvet Underground, and it is doing real work. A mirror does not interpret. It returns what is put in front of it, at the angle you choose to stand.

Forty years of the same photograph, and not one of them repeated.
