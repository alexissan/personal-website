Chapter I

Air

The aircraft was never the difficult part. Getting permission to sit in one was.

There is a pattern in these stories, and it is worth stating plainly at the outset because it recurs in every chapter that follows. The obstacle is almost never the mountain, the aircraft or the equation. It is an institution — a flight school, a university, a committee, a nomination form — and it has to be solved first, before anyone gets near the actual difficulty.

Which means the defining quality here is not courage in the adventure-story sense. It is a specific, unglamorous stubbornness about administrative obstacles: learning the language, finding the sponsor, insisting on the amendment.

§ 1 — Seven months of French

In post-war Chicago, Bessie Coleman could not get flight instruction. Not as a Black woman, not from anyone she asked. The barrier was administrative, so she solved it administratively: she took French classes at night, worked to save the fare, sailed to Europe, and in 1921 was issued a licence by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale.

She was the first Black woman and the first Native American woman in the world to hold one. She then had to invent a career, because no airline would employ her either — so she barnstormed. Loops, figure eights, parachute jumps. She refused to perform at any venue that segregated its entrance, and turned down work rather than compromise on it.

Being first says less about the person than about how long everyone else waited.
The recurring finding of this monograph

§ 2 — Passenger, then pilot

Amelia Earhart's first Atlantic crossing, in 1928, was as a passenger. She said so, repeatedly and irritably, and described herself as baggage on the trip — the fame arrived years before the achievement did.

In 1932 she corrected the record by flying it alone: Newfoundland to Ireland, through ice and a leaking fuel line and a failed altimeter. Three years later she flew Honolulu to California solo, a crossing that had already killed several pilots who attempted it and which she completed with fuel to spare.

She was also, less famously, a relentless organiser — a founder and first president of the Ninety-Nines, the international association of licensed women pilots, which still exists.

Chapter II

Element

Two prizes, in two sciences — and one committee that had to be corrected first.

Maria Skłodowska could not attend university in Warsaw, because Russian-controlled Poland did not admit women. She studied instead at the Flying University — an illegal, itinerant night school that moved between apartments to avoid the authorities — and then saved for years to reach Paris, where the Sorbonne would take her.

She arrived in 1891, enrolled as Marie, and lived on very little in an unheated attic. Within seven years she and Pierre Curie had isolated two new elements from several tonnes of pitchblende residue, processed by hand in a shed with a leaking roof.

§ 1 — On being left off the form

When the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics was first proposed for the work on radioactivity, the nomination named her collaborators and not her. Pierre Curie objected, and the committee amended it. She became the first woman to receive a Nobel Prize largely because someone with standing made a fuss at the right moment — a less inspiring mechanism than the story usually implies, and a more useful one to understand.

She won a second Prize in 1911, in Chemistry, alone, for the isolation of radium. She remains the only person ever to have won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences. In the interval between them, the French Academy of Sciences declined to elect her a member, by two votes.

She named the element after a country that was not on the map.
Polonium, 1898

§ 2 — The field ambulances

The part usually left out of the summary: in 1914 she stopped doing physics. Recognising that X-ray equipment existed but sat in hospitals far behind the front, she raised funds, had vehicles fitted out as mobile radiography units, learned to drive, and took them to field hospitals herself. Around twenty of these units operated during the war, with several hundred women trained to run them.

She and her daughter Irène worked at the front. The exposure, across decades of unshielded research and wartime radiography, eventually killed her — her notebooks from the period are still radioactive and are kept in lead-lined boxes.

Irène Joliot-Curie won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1935.

Chapter III

Altitude

Forty-eight orbits, one set of hand-checked trajectories, and an avalanche at Camp II.

In June 1963 Valentina Tereshkova flew Vostok 6 alone for almost three days — forty-eight orbits of the Earth. She was twenty-six. She had been recruited not as a pilot but as a parachutist, from an amateur club, while working in a textile mill, and trained for roughly a year.

The parachuting mattered more than it sounds. Vostok cosmonauts did not land inside the capsule; they ejected at around seven kilometres and came down under their own canopy. Which is precisely why the recruiters had gone looking in a parachute club.

§ 1 — The one who checked the machine

Katherine Johnson spent thirty-three years at NACA and then NASA computing trajectories by hand — launch windows, orbital insertions, emergency return paths. She worked in a segregated computing pool, and had to argue her way into the editorial meetings where the results she had produced were discussed.

When electronic computers took over the orbital calculations for John Glenn's 1962 Mercury flight, Glenn is reported to have asked that she personally verify the machine's output before he would fly. She did, and he went.

The mountain was never the hard part, and every one of them knew it.
Closing note

§ 2 — Twelve days after the avalanche

Junko Tabei's Everest expedition was funded partly by teaching piano lessons, because no sponsor would take an all-women climbing team seriously enough to write a cheque. At Camp II an avalanche buried the tents. She was dug out unconscious by a Sherpa guide.

Twelve days later she reached the summit. She went on to become the first woman to complete the Seven Summits, and spent much of the rest of her life working on the environmental damage that mass mountaineering had done to the routes she helped open.

None of the women in this monograph asked to be a first. It is a category imposed from outside, and a slightly insulting one — it describes the state of an institution rather than the ability of a person. What they have in common is more specific and less romantic: each of them identified the actual obstacle correctly, and then went at that instead of complaining about it.

Chapter IV

Exposure

The only figure in this monograph for whom the camera was not the obstacle but the instrument.

Diana Spencer belongs in this book awkwardly, and the awkwardness is the point. She was not first at anything technical. She flew nothing, calculated nothing, climbed nothing. She was nineteen when she was engaged, twenty when she married, and the institution she found herself inside was not a flight school or a nominating committee but a monarchy and the newspapers that fed on it.

What she worked out — faster than most people around her, and against their advice — was that the attention itself was an asset. Everyone else treated the cameras as weather to be endured. She treated them as a distribution channel.

§ 1 — One handshake, April 1987

At the opening of a dedicated HIV/AIDS unit at Middlesex Hospital, she shook the hand of a patient. No gloves, no mask, in front of photographers. It sounds like nothing now, which is precisely the measure of how much it did.

In 1987 a substantial part of the British public believed HIV could be transmitted by casual contact — a handshake, a cup, a shared room. Patients were being nursed by staff in full protective gear for reasons that had nothing to do with medicine. A single widely-syndicated photograph did more to correct that than a great deal of public health messaging had managed, because it did not argue with anyone. It simply showed the thing being done.

She did not argue with the public. She arranged to be photographed doing the thing.
The method, stated plainly

§ 2 — The minefield, January 1997

In Angola she walked a partially cleared minefield with the HALO Trust, in a visor and body armour, and then walked it a second time because the press pack had missed the shot. The images went everywhere within a day.

The reaction at home was not admiration. A junior government minister called her a loose cannon; others complained that a royal figure had no business intervening in a matter of defence policy. She continued anyway, and travelled to Bosnia in August to meet survivors.

The Mine Ban Treaty was signed in Ottawa that December, three and a half months after she died. It would be lazy to credit her with it — the campaign was years old and driven by people who had worked on it far longer. What is fair to say is that she moved it from a specialist concern to a subject the general public had a settled opinion about, and did so in roughly a fortnight, using nothing but her own visibility.

§ 3 — The bill

The arrangement was never symmetrical, and it should not be presented as though she simply mastered it. She was the most photographed woman alive, pursued continuously, and the same machinery she used for the handshake and the minefield followed her to a tunnel in Paris in August 1997.

The 1995 Panorama interview, in which she spoke about the marriage and her own health, was for years treated as the moment she took the narrative back. In 2021 an independent inquiry commissioned by the BBC found that the journalist had obtained it by deception, using faked documents to gain her trust. That finding does not erase what she said, but it does complicate the tidy version of the story, and the tidy version is not worth defending.

Which is why she closes this monograph rather than opening it. The others in these pages identified an institution standing between them and the work, and went around it. She identified an institution that had already decided what she was for, and turned its own apparatus — the lenses, the front pages, the appetite — toward a handshake and a minefield. It cost her a great deal, and she was thirty-six.
